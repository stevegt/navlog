## Decision under test

What is the tension between promise-based governance and use of force?

Governance exists partly to prevent breakdown into force. But force, or the
credible possibility of force, often sits in the background of real-world
governance from the beginning. The same tension appears in systems:

- promises, budgets, incentives, reputation, and aid try to keep cooperation
  alive,
- bans, revocations, throttles, exclusions, and operator override become the
  harder edge when softer coordination fails or is insufficient.

This TE treats governance and force as parallel analyses. It uses both the
broad survey and the early commons synthesis as co-equal inputs.

## Governance lens

Governance, in the broad sense, includes:

- norms,
- reciprocity,
- reputation,
- aid and subsidy,
- education and cultural maintenance,
- diplomacy and negotiation,
- metering and transparent accounting,
- graduated sanctions,
- institutional legitimacy.

In the system, analogs include:

- capability budgets,
- transparent resource accounting,
- warnings and soft throttles,
- routing preference changes,
- repair obligations,
- local trust scoring,
- operator cooperation and federation agreements.

Governance tries to keep actors inside a relationship that can continue.

## Force lens

Force, in the broad sense, includes more than physical violence. It includes
coercive acts that do not depend on the target's continued voluntary
participation.

Human examples:

- policing,
- seizure,
- embargo,
- imprisonment,
- military force,
- legally compelled compliance.

System analogs:

- hard revocation,
- unilateral blackholing or exclusion,
- key invalidation,
- transport cut-off,
- kernel kill or operator override,
- permanent ban without a negotiated remediation path.

Force is attractive because it is fast and decisive. It is dangerous because
it can destroy legitimacy, invite abuse, and centralize power.

## Parallel scenario analysis

### Scenario 1: steady-state cooperation

In a healthy commons, governance does most of the work.

Human side:

- people keep norms because the relationship is valuable,
- monitoring and correction remain lightweight,
- aid and education cost less than repression.

System side:

- budgets, quotas, trust history, and local policy are enough,
- most handlers and peers keep their promises,
- exclusions remain rare and exceptional.

In this scenario, force should remain in the background.

### Scenario 2: free-riding begins

A subset of actors starts extracting more value than they contribute.

Human side:

- warnings, public accountability, reciprocal pressure, and graduated
  sanctions often work better than immediate violence,
- if local institutions can still restore cooperation, force is usually too
  blunt.

System side:

- reduced budgets, throttling, temporary refusal, cost attribution, and
  routing downgrades are the natural first responses,
- hard bans are often premature unless abuse is clearly strategic or severe.

This is the strongest case for promise-based governance.

### Scenario 3: persistent strategic defector

An actor keeps exploiting the commons after softer measures fail.

Human side:

- eventually some institutions move from persuasion to policing or exclusion,
- the community tests whether its governance has real boundaries.

System side:

- revocation, hard bans, or transport cut-offs become more plausible,
- operators may isolate a handler, peer, or federation that is clearly toxic.

Here the tension becomes real: if the system has no hard edge, it can be
overrun. If the hard edge is too easy to invoke, governance becomes disguised
domination.

### Scenario 4: institutional capture

The governor itself becomes the problem.

Human side:

- regulators, police, or states may claim legitimacy while serving captured
  interests,
- force then protects enclosure rather than the commons.

System side:

- a kernel, platform operator, dominant handler, or registry may invoke
  safety and order while entrenching monopoly or suppressing rivals,
- hard revocation and routing exclusion can become tools of capture.

This is why force cannot be discussed only as a defense against abuse. It is
also a vector of enclosure.

### Scenario 5: emergency and external attack

Sometimes the commons faces an acute outside threat.

Human side:

- disaster response, policing, or military action may genuinely be necessary,
- but emergency powers have a long history of becoming permanent.

System side:

- blackholing an active attack, cutting off compromised handlers, or forcing
  operator overrides may be legitimate,
- but emergency controls can become the thin end of a permanent control
  wedge.

Here force may be necessary, but only if the system retains accountability,
review, and the ability to return to normal governance afterward.

## What this means for PromiseGrid

PromiseGrid cannot honestly pretend it will never use force-like measures.
Any real system eventually faces:

- refusal,
- exclusion,
- revocation,
- emergency override,
- boundary defense.

The real design question is not whether coercion exists. It is:

- who may invoke it,
- under what legitimacy,
- with what review or reversibility,
- after which softer governance stages have been tried,
- and how to prevent coercive tools from becoming enclosure tools.

## Promise-based governance strengths

Promise-based governance is strongest when:

- relationships are ongoing,
- local knowledge matters,
- repair is possible,
- legitimacy matters as much as throughput,
- one blunt central authority would be too destructive.

It encourages:

- plurality,
- negotiated adaptation,
- gradual response,
- and the possibility of preserving cooperation after conflict.

## Promise-based governance weaknesses

It is weaker when:

- actors are highly strategic and transient,
- attack is cheaper than repair,
- shared maintenance is already underfunded,
- one party can rapidly externalize large costs onto everyone else,
- no institution is willing or able to defend boundaries.

## Force strengths and weaknesses

Force is strongest when:

- rapid boundary defense is necessary,
- softer tools have failed,
- the target is not participating in good faith,
- delay would destroy the commons outright.

Force is weakest when:

- legitimacy is already fragile,
- the governing institution is itself captured,
- coercion would become a standing instrument of enclosure,
- the real problem is under-maintenance or bad incentives rather than active
  sabotage.

## Candidate implications without locking them

- The system probably needs a clear ladder from soft governance to hard
  refusal, rather than pretending every response is equivalent.
- Hard exclusion powers should probably remain local, accountable, and as
  reversible as the threat model allows.
- If the kernel has coercive powers, those powers should be narrow and
  explicit rather than smuggled in as ordinary routing or resource policy.
- Governance institutions outside the kernel matter just as much as kernel
  mechanics.

## Questions to carry forward

- Which system actions count as ordinary governance, and which count as force
  or coercion?
- Who is allowed to invoke hard exclusion or emergency override?
- What review, appeal, or reversibility should exist after force-like
  actions?
- How do we stop anti-abuse and emergency powers from becoming permanent
  enclosure tools?
- How much coercive backdrop is unavoidable even in a promise-oriented
  system?
